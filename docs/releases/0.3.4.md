# Teachouse 0.3.4 for Windows and Android

This is the beta that follows 0.3.3.
It is still a beta, so expect rough edges, and please say what you find.
The headline is the second round of things you told us were wrong: the marketplaces on a resource page ran off the side of a phone, the phone's tab bar and search were laid out for a computer, a message about a save was a black bar across the bottom of the screen, a picker lost your ticks if you tapped away, and a phone could not connect a marketplace at all.
Behind that, every run that finishes now tells you, on a Notifications page and, once we have switched the mail on, by email.
Almost all of this reaches you from the server, so a browser has it the moment it is published, and 0.3.4 is the app build that carries the same console to Windows and Android.

## What changed in the app

A phone can connect a marketplace.
In the app on a phone, the TPT and Tes cards on Marketplaces now offer Connect where they used to send you to a computer.
Pressing it takes the app's one window to that marketplace's own sign-in page, and when you have signed in the app files the login on the phone, exactly as a computer does, and brings you back to Marketplaces with one sentence saying what happened.
There are five of those sentences, one for each way a sign-in can end: it was saved, it ran out of time, you backed out of it, the page never opened, or the sign-in finished and could not be kept on this device.
That last one used to share a sentence with the page never opening, which told a phone that had been signed out of Teachouse mid-sign-in the opposite of what had happened; it now says to sign in to Teachouse again and press Connect once more.
Whether the marketplace is connected is still read from us and never from the sentence, so reloading the page or pressing back cannot repeat a message about a sign-in that finished minutes ago.
A computer's Connect, which opens the marketplace in a second window, has not changed.

The back gesture on Android walks back through what you were looking at.
Pressing back on a marketplace's sign-in page returns you to Marketplaces, and the app reads that as a sign-in you abandoned rather than one that failed.
Pressing back on a console screen moves you a screen rather than out of the app, and pressing back at the app's first screen leaves the app, instead of landing you on a page you never asked for that used to sit behind it.

A phone's Disconnect prompt says one more thing.
Disconnecting removes the marketplace's login from the phone, but the marketplace's own sign-in stays in the phone's browser where we cannot reach it, so connecting again may not ask you for a password.
The prompt says so before you agree.

## What changed in the console

Opening a resource shows one small tile per marketplace.
The tiles sit in a grid that wraps, four across on a computer and two across on a phone, in place of the stack of six-line cards that ran off a phone's edge.
Each tile carries the marketplace's own mark, a region tag where three Tes sites share one mark, the same status word in the same colour the Resources board uses, and one short clause of detail chosen for that state.
Everything the old card said is still there: the whole sentence is the tile's hover, and it is what a screen reader reads out.
Tapping a tile goes where its status points.
A marketplace that needs a sign-in opens Marketplaces scrolled to that marketplace's own card, a live listing opens on the marketplace in your browser, and a send in flight or a failure opens the run that carried it.
The cross-list and mark-listed controls sit at the foot of the tile they belong to, the form for attaching a listing sits below the grid under the name of the marketplace it writes to, and the foot of the panel carries the same note about the marks that Marketplaces already shows.
The Resources board's row chips draw the mark in place of the acronym as well, still capped at two on a phone.

Every link that used to drop you on a bare Marketplaces page now lands on the card it meant.
The card is outlined for a moment so you can see which one, and it sits clear of the header band.

On a phone the console is laid out for a phone.
The strip across the top is gone, so each page's own heading starts about sixty-five pixels higher.
The tab bar at the foot now has six cells: the five sections and, at the end, a tile with your organisation's initials, labelled Account, which opens Settings.
The labels are smaller to make room, and on a phone 360 pixels wide or wider none is cut short.

Search on a phone opens where the keyboard leaves room.
The search sheet is placed against the band of screen the keyboard has left visible, with its head about a third of the way down that band, and it grows downward only, so the box you are typing in holds still as matches arrive.
On a browser that cannot say where the keyboard is, the sheet sits a gutter below the top of the screen as it did.
Search on a computer is unchanged.

A message about something that just happened is a light card near the top of the screen.
It is centred, at most 420 pixels wide on a computer and 24 pixels short of the screen's edges on a phone, drawn on the console's own surface with its usual rounded corners.
A green check leads a save, a red alert leads a refusal, and a muted Close sits at the trailing edge.
It replaces the black bar pinned to the bottom-right corner, which on a phone drew across the tab bar.
On Android the card sits below the status bar rather than under it.
When two messages are up, the newest is the one nearest the top, and on a message that runs to several lines the mark sits level with the first line rather than floating in the middle.

A picker keeps what you ticked when you tap away.
The pickers on the resource form, and the two on the Templates board, now close on a press anywhere outside their own box and on Escape, keeping every tick, because each tick had already reached the draft and a seller who tapped away to read the next field used to lose all of them.
Done is still there, as a filled button inside the picker under the last option, and it reads how many you have chosen: Done, 3 chosen.
A picker opens on a press, on typing, or on the down arrow or Enter, and never merely because the box was focused, so closing one no longer reopens it in the same instant.
Enter inside a picker chooses; it no longer submits the whole resource form.
On a phone a picker is a sheet at the bottom edge over a dimmed page, above the tab bar, with its search box kept above the dimming so the keyboard goes on filtering the list, and Done stretched across its last row.

Marketplaces draws the browsers' and platforms' own marks.
The Firefox tile carries Mozilla's Firefox lockup in colour under the heading Firefox browser, which is the form Mozilla asks for, and the Android download card carries Google's Android robot in colour.
The Chrome tile is headed Google Chrome, with the trademark symbol, while the box beside it keeps the shorter Chrome, and the Android card is headed Android with its symbol, because Google asks for it at the name's first appearance.
Five attribution sentences sit under the disclaimer as one block of fine print, and the disclaimer now covers browser and platform names as well as marketplaces.
The Apple card is headed macOS, since that is the only Apple platform a build could exist for.
The Android card says what the phone actually does: it holds marketplace logins and runs queued work when you open it, and it keeps no schedule of its own.
The Windows tile's wording says plainly that no licence for the Windows mark has been requested.
In a browser, the Marketplaces cards now say a login can be held on a computer or a phone.

Every run that finishes now tells you.
Notifications stops being a placeholder, and its entry in the menu drops the soon tag.
It lists every finished sync, migration and import, newest first, with its counts drawn in the same words and colours the outcome bar uses, so a run reads the same here as it does on the Sync page.
Each row names the inventory rather than the marketplace, so two Tes sites do not read alike, and opens the run's own page when you press it.
A run that changed nothing reads Nothing to change rather than an empty strip.
A migration is one row, not one for each of its two legs, and an import you re-commit on a sheet already in the catalogue is told forty skipped rather than forty succeeded.
Rows you have not seen carry a dot and the word New for a screen reader; opening the page marks them read, and the dots stay for the rest of the visit so you can see which ones were new.
Load more continues the list, and if that fails, the rows you already have stay on screen with the message beneath them.

You can ask for the same by email.
Preferences gains a Notifications panel with one switch, Email me when a run finishes, and a sentence naming the address it will go to, which is the address you sign in with.
The email carries the run's counts and one button to the run's page, and its subject says how many of the run's resources were updated, nine of eleven where anything did not succeed, rather than a total that reads as work done.
It is sent only when a run changed something, so a run that changed nothing fills the Notifications page and sends no mail.
Your address is asked of the sign-in service for the length of one send and is never stored beside your resources.
Whether any email goes out at all depends on the mail relay being switched on, which it is not yet in this release; see Known limitations.

## What changed on the site

The migration price table on the front page reads as two columns again, the label at the left edge and the price at the right with a hairline between rows.
This is the previous release's rendering restored, not a new one: the marketplace strip added in 0.3.2 had quietly overridden the table's styling.

Every mark in the marketplace strip draws in its owner's own colours at rest.
The greyscale filter and the recolour on hover are gone, because a reader on a phone cannot hover and a filter alters a drawing its owner published; the pill under a mark answers a pointer with a background change instead.

Etsy and Shopify join the strip as marks rather than names.
Boom Learning stays an initial, because its rule cannot be met truthfully.

Under the sentence about the small app there is now a platform row.
Windows appears by name alone, and Android appears as Google's robot in colour beside the name in its trademark form, with the attribution sentences as a caption.

## Known limitations

Email about a finished run is not yet being sent.
The mail relay Teachouse sends through has not been switched on, so until it is the switch in Preferences changes nothing and the Notifications page is the one place a finished run is announced.

Nothing in the menu counts your unread notifications.
The Notifications page is the only place that shows which runs you have not seen.

Connecting a marketplace from a phone has been exercised on an emulator and in the app's own tests, not yet on a phone in your hand.
The same is true of the back gesture: no handset has yet been seen pressing back on a marketplace's sign-in page, and if the app closes instead of returning you to Marketplaces, that is the first thing to tell us.
In a browser, connecting a marketplace still has to be done in the app, on a computer or on a phone.

Pressing back on Android with a picker or the search sheet open moves you a screen rather than closing the sheet.

The tab bar at the foot still sits under the gesture pill on a phone that navigates by gestures.
On a phone narrower than 360 pixels, three of its labels are cut short.

Publishing an imported batch to a marketplace is still a separate step.
The import creates the resources and stops there; sending them on is the ordinary send, run from your own device, and the import does not start it for you.

Boom Learning appears in the front page's band by its initial rather than its logo, and Windows appears in the platform row by name alone.
Each needs written permission before its mark may be used, and neither has been asked for yet.

There is no dark theme yet.
The colours are defined once in one place, which is what a dark theme needs, but only the light one is built.

The emails about signing in, verifying an address and resetting a password are still in the older colours.
The new email about a finished run is drawn in the console's own colours, so for now the two do not match.

Registering a phone as a machine has still been exercised on an emulator and in the app's own tests, not yet on a phone signed in to a live account.
Your phone is still the first live check, and Your machines is where it should appear.

Following a marketplace link out to your own browser has been exercised in the app's own tests, not yet on a phone.

The hourly limit on how often a phone goes looking for marketplace work has been exercised in the app's own tests, not yet on a phone.

Editing several resources at once is still not built.
Editing them one at a time works, and the board says so where each bulk control would be.

Marketplace Sharing is laid out and does nothing.
There is no browser extension for Chrome or Firefox, and no Apple build.
No marketplace beyond Tes and TPT is connected.
A subscription cannot be bought yet.
The privacy and terms pages are still drafts.

## Updating

If you are on 0.3.3 on Windows, this arrives on its own.
The app checks the beta channel each time it opens and installs a new version before it starts working.

The Android app does not update itself.
Download the package from this release and install it over the one you have.

The console needs no update at all.
It is served from Teachouse, so it is already what you see in a browser and what the app shows you when it opens, with nothing to install for it.

## Installing it

Windows will warn you that the publisher is unknown.
That is expected for now: this build is not yet signed with a code-signing certificate, and the warning will go away in a later release.

## For operators

One migration lands with this release.
Migration 0063 creates `notification`: tenant data under forced row-level security, one row per run held by a unique index over the organisation, the kind and the run, a keyset index for the list, and the six settled counts as one `jsonb` document composed when the run finished.
It adds `app_user.notify_email`, the per-user mail switch, defaulting on.
It adds `job.sync_request_id`, one immutable column with a partial index, written by the same statement that mints the job, so a settle landing between the job's creation and the request's later recording of it still finds its run.
It adds `import_batch_row.skipped`, so an import counts the rows a pass claimed and did not create apart from the ones it created.
The tenant matrix gains its forty-third table.

The grants to `tam_engine` are the narrowest that make the settle work.
On `notification` it holds `INSERT` alone, because nothing on an engine path reads or marks a row and a cross-tenant role holding `UPDATE` could mark every organisation's inbox read.
On `app_user` it holds a column-scoped `SELECT` over `id`, `org_id`, `auth_subject` and `notify_email`, with `email` left out because that column holds a placeholder; this is the first privilege the engine role has held on a user table.
On `sync_request` it holds a column-scoped `SELECT` over the six columns the settle names, `org_id`, `id`, `disposition`, `target`, `create_job_id` and `remove_job_id`, because the role bypasses the policy and not the privilege, and a table-wide grant would have handed it every organisation's `failure_detail`, which is free text a marketplace's own error lands in.
Two tests pin that boundary: one asserts the six columns are readable, the other that insert, update and delete are each refused and the other six columns each unreadable.
One consequence for any later writer on `notification`: Postgres serves `ON CONFLICT` only to a role that also holds `SELECT`, so an `INSERT`-only role cannot use it, and the inbox write is a plain insert whose unique violation is caught and read as another writer having got there first.

Four routes land, moving the route table from ninety-one to ninety-five:

- `GET /{version}/notifications?cursor&limit`: the inbox newest first behind an opaque cursor; the list asks for one row past its limit, so a page that happens to be exactly full mints no cursor.
- `POST /{version}/notifications/read`: marks every unread row of the organisation at or before the named one, in the list's own order; answers zero the second time, and 404 for a row another organisation holds, which is the same answer an unknown row gets.
- `GET` and `PATCH /{version}/notifications/preferences`: the requesting user's own mail switch; the body has no field with which to name another user.

All four refuse without a session, and the notification kind crosses to the console through the generated vocabulary.

The identity service gains one internal route, `/internal/address/{subject}`, answering the address, its verified flag and the name for a subject it already owns to a caller offering the shared secret in the `x-tam-internal-secret` header.
The secret is compared over SHA-256 digests in constant time, so its length is not an oracle.
The route exists only where `TAM_AUTH_INTERNAL_SECRET` is set: with no secret the path answers the same 404 as every unknown path, and so does a wrong secret.
The production proxy forwards only the auth prefix to that service and the service binds to loopback, so the route is reachable only from the host the drainer runs on, and the secret is the fence either way.
Any further route on that service is a new founder decision, not an application of this one.

`tam-server` takes five new values, given together or not at all; four of the five refuses the start.

- `--resend-api-key-file <path>`: the Resend API key, read from a bounded file.
- `--email-from <address>`: the sender the Resend account owns.
- `--console-url <url>`: the console's public origin, which the mail's button resolves against; stated rather than derived from `--auth-issuer`, because nothing holds the two to one origin.
- `--auth-internal-url <url>`: where the identity service's internal route is reached, `http://127.0.0.1:8081` in the current deployment shape.
- `--auth-internal-secret-file <path>`: the shared secret, read from a bounded file, matching `TAM_AUTH_INTERNAL_SECRET` in the identity service's environment.

The two secrets are files rather than inline flags so that neither appears in a process listing.
With none of the five present the drainer selects the logging deliverer and behaves byte for byte as it did, which is what development and CI run.
The deployment module does not yet carry options for these five values or for the identity service's secret, so switching the mail on in production is a deployment change that follows this release, and the first real send is a founder act after the Resend key lands; nothing in this release has contacted Resend.

The relay's answer is classified before the attempt budget is spent.
A 429 and a 408 are retried ahead of the branch that dead-letters every other client error, because a drain pass posts up to thirty-two messages back to back and the first 429 would otherwise have spent a mail's whole budget in one pass.
An address the identity service will not vouch for is dead-lettered on the first attempt, because retrying cannot verify it, and the subject is logged when that happens; a subject that never verified its address simply receives no mail.
Nothing reads a dead letter yet: a mail that exhausts its twelve attempts is a line in the drainer's log and nothing else.

The reserved organisation slugs gain `vendors`, the fifty-fourth, in the server's list and the console's mirror, because the console now serves a directory of that name for the browser and platform marks and an organisation holding it would shadow it.

The landing lane gains a style gate.
The build fails when a selector is declared twice at the top level of the stylesheet, keyed on each selector rather than on the whole list, with one narrowing allowed: a later rule passes only where its whole list is a strict subset of the list that last declared the selector.
Quoted strings are blanked beside comments so a brace inside one cannot desynchronise the walk, and a floor of eighty rules stops a scan that read nothing from passing.
The one genuine clash it found in the tree, which was the migration table's, is merged.
