Every claim below was read on 2026-09-05 and carries the URL it came from.

## How to read the verification marks

`(fetched)` means the cited page was retrieved and read directly on 2026-09-05.
`(index)` means the cited page blocked automated retrieval and the quoted text reached me through a search engine's extract of that same page on 2026-09-05, so the wording is second-hand and should be re-read by a human before it is relied on in published copy.
`(unverified)` means only a third-party account exists and the primary page was not read.

A finding worth stating up front: the brief assumed Amazon Ignite had closed, and it has not.
It is live and invitation-only, so it belongs in the live list with a caveat rather than the closed list.

## Trademark note

The general rule that covers what we are doing is nominative or referential use: you may use another party's mark to refer to that party's own goods or services, so long as you use no more of the mark than you need and do nothing that suggests they sponsor or endorse you.

The International Trademark Association states the doctrine as permitting use of another's mark "to refer to the trademark owner's goods and services associated with the mark", subject to three conditions, quoted verbatim from https://www.inta.org/fact-sheets/fair-use-of-trademarks-intended-for-a-non-legal-audience/ (fetched): "the product or service in question is not readily identifiable without use of the trademark"; "only so much of the mark as is reasonably necessary to identify the product or service is used"; and "use of the mark does not suggest sponsorship or endorsement by the trademark owner".
The same three-part test originates in New Kids on the Block v. News America Publishing, Inc., 971 F.2d 302 (9th Cir. 1992), reported at https://openjurist.org/971/f2d/302/new-kids-on-the-block-v-news-america-publishing-inc-usa-new-kids-on-the-block, which requires that "the product or service in question must be one not readily identifiable without use of the trademark", that "only so much of the mark or marks may be used as is reasonably necessary to identify the product or service", and that "the user must do nothing that would, in conjunction with the mark, suggest sponsorship or endorsement by the trademark holder" (index).

In the European Union the equivalent is Article 14 of Regulation (EU) 2017/1001, read at https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32017R1001 (fetched).
Article 14(1)(c) permits a third party to use "the EU trade mark for the purpose of identifying or referring to goods or services as those of the proprietor", and Article 14(2) conditions that on the use being "in accordance with honest practices" in industrial or commercial matters.

In the United Kingdom the equivalent is section 11(2)(c) of the Trade Marks Act 1994, read at https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1994/26/section/11 (fetched), which excludes from infringement use "for the purpose of identifying or referring to goods or services as those of the proprietor of that trade mark", again "provided the use is in accordance with honest practices in industrial or commercial matters".

Three consequences for the tiles.
First, naming each marketplace is squarely within the rule, because there is no other way to say which marketplace a tile is about.
Second, several owners impose rules stricter than the law allows them to enforce, and Etsy and Shopify both require written permission before their logos are used at all, so the safe default across the whole grid is a wordmark set in our own typeface, with an owner's supplied logo used only where that owner's own rules permit it or permission has been granted in writing.
Third, we should carry a disclaimer under the grid along the lines that the marketplace names and marks shown belong to their respective owners, that we are not affiliated with or endorsed by them, and that the names identify marketplaces only; Etsy's own suggested wording is a usable model.

Nothing here is legal advice, and the two conclusions with the most exposure — logo use on the Etsy and Shopify tiles, and the wording of the grid disclaimer — are the ones worth putting to a solicitor before launch.
